In Conversation with Camus and Durkheim: Musings on Suicide

8 min read

There are few questions as uncomfortable as the question of whether life is worth living. It is uncomfortable not merely because of the suffering contained within it, but because it seems to demand an answer to something more fundamental: what makes life valuable in the first place? If existence does not come with an inherent meaning, why continue to exist? And if meaning can disappear, what holds a person to life when the reasons for living no longer seem convincing?

Albert Camus begins The Myth of Sisyphus with precisely this problem. He famously describes suicide as the one truly serious philosophical problem, because before asking how one ought to live, philosophy must first confront whether life is worth living at all. Camus's interest, however, is not simply in suicide as an act. He is interested in the confrontation between human beings and what he calls the absurd: the tension between humanity's desire for order, meaning, and intelligibility and a world that offers no guaranteed answer to those desires. We ask for meaning, and yet the universe remains silent.

Camus ultimately refuses to treat suicide as a philosophical solution to this silence. Instead, he proposes a kind of rebellion: to recognize the absence of an ultimate guaranteed meaning and nevertheless continue living. For him, the absurd does not necessarily make life worthless. It makes the question of value unavoidable.

Yet Camus's question remains largely existential. It concerns the individual confronted with existence itself. Suicide, however, is not only a philosophical question. It is also a psychological experience, a social phenomenon, and a historical reality. People do not experience despair in a vacuum. They exist within families, communities, institutions, economies, cultures, and relationships. Their sense of isolation or belonging is partly shaped by the world around them.

It is here that Émile Durkheim provides a radically different perspective.

Durkheim's Suicide (1897) challenged the assumption that suicide could be explained primarily through the private characteristics of the individual. Rather than asking only why a particular person might wish to die, Durkheim asked why rates of suicide differed systematically between societies and social groups. His argument was that suicide, despite appearing to be one of the most individual acts imaginable, could be studied as a social fact.

While Camus asks, in effect, "Why live?" Durkheim asks a different question: "What happens between an individual and society that makes life more or less connected, regulated, and sustainable?" Where Camus looks inward toward the individual's confrontation with existence, Durkheim looks outward toward the individual's relationship with other people.

Durkheim's central concepts are social integration and social regulation. Integration refers broadly to the strength of a person's attachment to social groups and communities. Regulation refers to the degree to which social norms, expectations, and institutions provide structure for people's desires and lives. For Durkheim, both extremes can become problematic. A person can be insufficiently connected to society, but they can also be absorbed into it to such an extent that the individual self becomes secondary. Likewise, social life can provide too little structure, but it can also become excessively restrictive.

From these dimensions, Durkheim developed four ideal types of suicide: egoistic, altruistic, anomic, and fatalistic.

Egoistic suicide emerges from insufficient social integration. The individual becomes detached from the groups and relationships that ordinarily provide a sense of belonging and purpose. The problem is not simply loneliness in the ordinary sense. It is a deeper weakening of the social bonds through which an individual understands themselves as part of something larger.

Altruistic suicide represents the opposite extreme: excessive integration. Here, the individual is so deeply incorporated into a collective identity that personal existence can become subordinate to the perceived needs or values of the group. The self becomes so identified with the collective that sacrificing the individual life can be understood as an obligation.

Anomic suicide concerns regulation rather than integration. Durkheim uses the concept of anomie to describe situations in which established norms and expectations are disrupted. Human desires, according to Durkheim, are not naturally self-limiting. Social institutions help provide boundaries and expectations that make life intelligible. When those structures suddenly weaken — through major social or economic disruption, for example — individuals may experience a profound instability in what they can reasonably expect from life.

Finally, Durkheim describes fatalistic suicide as associated with excessive regulation: a situation in which the individual experiences social rules and constraints as overwhelmingly restrictive. Although this category receives considerably less attention in his work than the others, it completes the conceptual framework. If anomie represents too little regulation, fatalism represents too much.

The importance of these categories lies less in their ability to classify every individual case than in the question behind them. Durkheim is asking us to consider whether individual suffering can be separated from the structure of social life.

This is where his theory becomes particularly powerful.

Suicide is often imagined as an intensely private act. The person is the one experiencing the pain, making the decision, and ultimately acting. From this perspective, the explanation naturally seems to lie within the individual: their mental state, their memories, their trauma, their beliefs or their perception of the future. Durkheim does not necessarily deny the existence of these individual experiences. Instead, he argues that an exclusively individual explanation is incomplete.

A person is never simply an isolated consciousness.

A person's sense of self is constructed partly through relationships. Family, friendship, work, education, religion, community, and culture all contribute to the ways in which people understand who they are and where they belong. Social institutions provide roles and expectations. Relationships provide recognition. Communities provide continuity. When these structures are weakened, the resulting experience is not merely "social" in an abstract sense. It can become deeply personal.

This is perhaps the most significant insight offered by Durkheim: social conditions can become psychological experiences.

Isolation is social, but it is also felt. Anomie is structural, but it can be experienced as confusion or hopelessness. Excessive regulation is institutional, but it can be experienced as entrapment. Belonging is social, but it can become part of a person's fundamental sense that their existence matters to other people.

The distinction between society and the individual therefore becomes less straightforward than it initially appears.

Nevertheless, Durkheim's theory has important limitations. The fact that social conditions influence suicide does not mean that they determine it. Individuals living under similar circumstances can respond in radically different ways. Two people may experience the same economic crisis, the same degree of social isolation, or the same disruption in their lives, yet one may experience it as temporary while another experiences it as unbearable.

This is where psychological approaches become indispensable.

A sociological theory can explain why certain environments may increase vulnerability, but it cannot fully explain the subjective experience of an individual. Psychological states such as hopelessness, severe emotional distress, trauma, or other forms of mental suffering cannot simply be reduced to social structure. The individual does not merely occupy a social position; they interpret it. They remember, anticipate, fear, desire, and assign meaning to what happens to them.

In other words, Durkheim can help explain the conditions surrounding suffering, while psychology can help explain the experience of suffering itself.

This distinction also brings the discussion back to Camus.

Camus and Durkheim appear, at first, to be asking entirely different questions. Camus begins with the individual standing before the universe and asking whether existence is worth continuing. Durkheim begins with society and asks what happens when the bonds and structures connecting individuals to that society become too weak or too strong.

Yet, the two perspectives can be placed into conversation. Camus's absurd individual is searching for meaning in a world that does not provide an obvious, universal answer. Durkheim's individual is embedded in a social world whose institutions and relationships can provide (or fail to provide) belonging and structure. One emphasizes existential meaning; the other emphasizes social connection.

The relationship between these ideas becomes particularly interesting when we consider that meaning itself is rarely produced in isolation.

Human beings often derive meaning through other people. Identity is relational. Purpose can emerge from responsibility, friendship, family, work, creativity, community, or a sense of contributing to something beyond oneself. Even when meaning is experienced privately, the language and concepts through which people understand that meaning are socially inherited.

This does not invalidate Camus's philosophy. Instead, it complicates it.

Perhaps the question "Is life worth living?" cannot be answered entirely at the level of abstract philosophy because people rarely experience life as an abstract philosophical proposition. They experience it through concrete relationships and circumstances. A person's answer to the question of whether life is worth living may change as their relationships change, as their circumstances change, or as their understanding of themselves changes.

Likewise, Durkheim's sociology cannot provide the entire answer. Society can shape the conditions in which suffering occurs, but society does not mechanically produce a particular response in every individual. The same social world can be experienced differently by different people.

This suggests that suicide is better understood not through a single theory but through an interaction between levels of explanation.

At the broadest level, there is society: institutions, economic conditions, cultural expectations, communities, social inequality, and changing forms of belonging. At the individual level, there is psychology: perception, memory, emotion, trauma, hopelessness, and the interpretation of one's circumstances. And beneath — or perhaps alongside — both is the philosophical question raised by Camus: what makes existence meaningful enough to continue?

These levels should not be treated as mutually exclusive.

The mistake would be to say that suicide is either a social problem or a psychological one. It can be both. Social circumstances can contribute to psychological suffering, while psychological suffering can alter the way an individual experiences their social circumstances. Philosophy enters the picture because human beings do not merely experience pain; they attempt to understand what that pain means.

Camus begins with the individual and expands the question outward toward existence itself. If there is no predetermined meaning, what does it mean to choose life? His answer is not to discover a final explanation that makes suffering disappear. It is to acknowledge uncertainty and still participate in existence.

Between these two thinkers lies a useful tension.

Camus reminds us that no society can completely answer the individual's existential questions. Durkheim reminds us that no individual exists completely outside society.

The first prevents sociology from becoming overly deterministic. The second prevents existential philosophy from becoming overly isolated. Together, they suggest that understanding suicide requires us to resist the temptation of a single explanation.

There is no universal psychological profile that can account for every case, just as there is no social structure that mechanically determines an individual's fate. Human beings are simultaneously individuals and members of groups. They experience their lives internally, but the conditions of those lives are partly external.

The question, therefore, is not simply whether suicide is caused by the individual or by society. That division is too simple. A better question is how the individual and society continually shape one another.

How does social isolation become loneliness? How does social instability become personal uncertainty? How does belonging become a reason to continue? How do cultural expectations influence the meaning a person assigns to suffering? And, ultimately, how does an individual's interpretation of their existence emerge from the complicated intersection between their inner world and the world around them?

Camus begins with the question of whether life is worth living. Durkheim demonstrates that the answer cannot be separated entirely from how people live together. Psychology reminds us that social circumstances are filtered through individual experience. None of these perspectives is sufficient by itself.

Perhaps that is precisely why suicide remains such a difficult subject to theorize. It sits at the boundary between the private and the public, between consciousness and structure, between meaning and circumstance. It forces philosophy to confront the vulnerability of the individual, sociology to confront the consequences of collective life, and psychology to confront the complexity of human suffering.

The theoretical task, then, is not to find one final cause. It is to understand the relationships between these different dimensions without reducing one to another.

Camus's Sisyphus continues pushing his stone not because the universe finally reveals a purpose to him, but because he chooses to confront the absurdity of existence. Durkheim, looking not at the individual stone but at the society surrounding the individual, asks what happens when the bonds that give human beings a place within the world begin to weaken or become oppressive.

Between them is a picture of the human being that is neither entirely alone nor entirely determined by society.

The individual searches for meaning. Society provides (or fails to provide) belonging and structure.

And somewhere between the two, lies the complicated, often painful experience of being alive.
